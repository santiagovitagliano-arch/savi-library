How to Go to Confession (After Years Away)

A gentle, complete walkthrough — what to say, what to expect, the prayers you need.

If you have not been to confession in years — or ever — this guide walks you through everything you need to know to do it well. The structure of the sacrament, the words to say, the questions to expect, and the only prayer you absolutely have to know. The doors open in. The most-feared part is not as hard as you imagine.

First and most important: the priest has heard everything. There is no sin you can bring that has not been brought before, repeatedly, by people in worse shape than you. He is not there to judge you; he is there to extend the mercy of Christ. The whole point of his vocation is to do exactly what you are about to ask him to do.

Before You Go

Find the parish and the times

Most parishes hear confessions on Saturday afternoons (4:00 to 5:00 PM is the most common window) and sometimes before weekday Masses. Check your parish website or call. If your local parish times do not work, any Catholic parish anywhere can hear your confession. You do not need to be a registered member.

Examine your conscience

Spend ten to twenty minutes reviewing your life since your last confession. Use the Ten Commandments and the seven capital sins as a guide. For a thorough walkthrough, see our companion piece on examples of sins to say at confession. Write things down if it helps. You can bring the note into the confessional.

Know the Act of Contrition

Memorize it if you can. If you cannot, bring it on a printed card or your phone. The short form is enough: "My God, I am sorry for my sins with all my heart. In choosing to do wrong and failing to do good, I have sinned against You whom I should love above all things. I firmly intend, with Your help, to do penance, to sin no more, and to avoid whatever leads me to sin. Amen." For the full prayer and its history, see the Act of Contrition.

The Seven Steps Inside the Confessional

Step 1 Enter

You can choose to confess face-to-face or behind a screen (anonymously). Both are valid. Pick whichever you are comfortable with. Kneel or sit. Make the Sign of the Cross. Wait for the priest to greet you.

Step 2 Open

The traditional opening: "Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been [length of time] since my last confession." If you don't remember when you last confessed, simply say "It has been many years" or "I do not remember." That is fine.

Step 3 Confess

Name your sins by kind. For grave matter, give a rough number. The format is simple:

  • "I missed Mass on Sunday several times."
  • "I lied to my spouse."
  • "I have used pornography on multiple occasions."
  • "I held anger against my brother for a long time."
  • "I have neglected daily prayer."

Do not narrate. Do not tell the story. Name the sin and move on. If you forget something, mention it at the end. If you forget something completely and remember it later, mention it next time — your confession is still valid.

Step 4 Listen

The priest may say nothing, or may offer brief counsel. Most priests give a sentence or two. Receive it as a gift even if it does not feel particularly profound. He is speaking from years of hearing confessions.

Step 5 Receive your penance

The priest will give you a penance. Usually a small number of prayers (three Our Fathers, a decade of the rosary, an Act of Contrition) or sometimes a specific act (read a particular Scripture passage, do something kind for the person you have wronged). It will be reasonable. Accept it and remember it.

Step 6 Pray the Act of Contrition

Pray the Act of Contrition aloud or silently. If you forget the words, the priest will help you, or you can simply say in your own words that you are sorry for your sins and intend, with God's grace, to amend your life. Sincerity is what matters.

Step 7 Receive absolution

The priest will say the words of absolution: "God, the Father of mercies, through the death and resurrection of his Son has reconciled the world to himself and sent the Holy Spirit among us for the forgiveness of sins; through the ministry of the Church may God give you pardon and peace, and I absolve you from your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." Make the Sign of the Cross. He will dismiss you, usually with "Go in peace." You may respond "Thanks be to God" or simply "Thank you, Father."

Common Questions Answered Honestly

What if I cry?

Many people do. Bring a tissue. The priest will wait. Tears in the confessional are old news.

What if I run out of time?

Most confessions take five to ten minutes once you are inside. If there is a long line on a Saturday afternoon, the priest may move you along briskly, but he will not cut you off if you have grave matter to bring. If the line is impossibly long, return another day.

What if I cannot remember my sins?

Bring what you can remember. Confess what is real. What you do not remember is forgiven along with what you do, as long as your sorrow is for all of it.

What if I am unsure whether something is a sin?

Mention it. Say: "I am not sure whether this is a sin, but I want to bring it." The priest will help you understand it and respond appropriately.

What if I am embarrassed by what I have to say?

Embarrassment is not a reason to withhold. Withholding makes the confession invalid. Better to be embarrassed for two minutes in front of one priest than to carry it for another year.

How often should I go?

The Church requires at least once per year if you are in a state of grave sin. The recommended practice is monthly, regardless. Many of the saints went weekly. Find a rhythm and keep it.

After You Leave

Perform your penance as soon as is practical, ideally before the day ends. Do not delay it — the penance is the first act of the new life you have just been given.

Then go to Mass. Receive Communion. Pray. Continue. The sacrament is not a finish line; it is a starting line. The work that follows is the slow practice of becoming someone who needs less confession over time — not by hiding sins better but by sinning less in the first place. For the longer reading on the inner work this requires, see our companion guides on spiritual awakening and contemplative prayer.

The doors really do open in. The priest is waiting. The mercy is real.
