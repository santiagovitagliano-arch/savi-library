The Act of Contrition: Full Text, Meaning, and How to Pray It

The traditional prayer, the modern form, and how to say it from the heart.

The Act of Contrition is the prayer of sorrow at the heart of Catholic confession. It is said in the confessional after the priest has heard your sins and before he gives absolution. It is also prayed at the end of each day as part of an examination of conscience. This guide gives you the full text in English (both traditional and modern), what each line actually means, and what to do if the words leave you in the moment.

I. What is the Act of Contrition

The Act of Contrition is one of the oldest formal prayers in the Catholic tradition. Its name is exact: an act, not a description. To pray it is to do something, not to say something about sorrow. The prayer puts sorrow into action by combining three movements at once: an admission that you have sinned, sorrow for the sin itself, and a firm purpose of amendment going forward.

The Catechism teaches that contrition is "sorrow of the soul and detestation for the sin committed, together with the resolution not to sin again." The prayer gives that interior movement a body of words.

Two kinds of sorrow are distinguished in the tradition. Perfect contrition is sorrow that arises from love of God for His own sake. Imperfect contrition (sometimes called attrition) arises from a more mixed motive: fear of consequences, awareness of having broken something good, awareness of the ugliness of sin itself. Both are valid material for the sacrament. The grace of the sacrament can purify imperfect sorrow into something fuller.

II. The Traditional Act of Contrition (English)

Traditional Form
O my God, I am heartily sorry for having offended Thee, and I detest all my sins because of Thy just punishments, but most of all because they offend Thee, my God, who art all good and deserving of all my love. I firmly resolve, with the help of Thy grace, to sin no more and to avoid the near occasions of sin. Amen.

This is the form most Catholics over forty learned by heart. The archaic Thee and Thou are not religious window-dressing. They are the older English second-person singular, the intimate form: the form one used with God, with one's family, with a close friend. The modern You is the formal plural form that English eventually flattened into universal use. The traditional prayer addresses God the way a child addresses a father at the kitchen table.

III. The Modern Act of Contrition (English)

Modern Form (Rite of Penance)
My God, I am sorry for my sins with all my heart. In choosing to do wrong and failing to do good, I have sinned against You whom I should love above all things. I firmly intend, with Your help, to do penance, to sin no more, and to avoid whatever leads me to sin. Our Savior Jesus Christ suffered and died for us. In His name, my God, have mercy. Amen.

This is the form printed in the current Rite of Penance and used most often in confession today. It says the same thing as the traditional prayer in plainer modern English, and adds an explicit naming of Christ's sacrifice. Both forms are completely valid. Use whichever your memory carries, or whichever the parish you attend uses.

IV. What Each Line Actually Means

"I am sorry for my sins with all my heart"

The sorrow has to be of the whole self. Not regret that you got caught. Not embarrassment. Not even the wish that things had gone differently. Sorrow for the sin itself, because of what it is.

"In choosing to do wrong and failing to do good"

The prayer names both kinds of sin: commission (the wrong I chose) and omission (the good I failed to do). Most examinations of conscience focus only on the first. The second is often larger.

"I have sinned against You whom I should love above all things"

Every sin is at root a refusal to love God. Other persons may be wounded by what we do; the deepest wound is always in the relationship that grounds all other relationships. This is the line where perfect contrition can rise: when the sorrow is genuinely for God's sake, not for your own.

"I firmly intend, with Your help"

The intention has to be firm. Not "I'll try." Not "I hope to do better." The Latin original is firmiter propono: I firmly propose. And it has to be with God's help — not a Pelagian boast of moral strength but a sober admission that you cannot do this alone.

"To do penance, to sin no more, and to avoid whatever leads me to sin"

Three parts. Do penance: accept the satisfaction the priest gives you and offer something further if grace prompts it. Sin no more: the simple resolution. Avoid whatever leads me to sin: the practical part, which is often the part most neglected. The drinker who resolves not to drink but stays in the bar has not fully prayed the prayer.

V. When to Pray the Act of Contrition

There are four ordinary times to pray it.

  • During confession, after the priest has heard your sins, often immediately before absolution. He may ask you to pray it aloud, silently, or in your own words.
  • At the end of each day, as the closing prayer of an examination of conscience. Many spiritual masters recommend this nightly practice. Five minutes of review, a brief prayer of sorrow, a hand of release.
  • In the moment of recognizing a sin. When something happens during the day and you become aware that you have failed, a brief Act of Contrition right then can be made silently, anywhere. It is not a substitute for confession of grave matter. It is an immediate turning back.
  • As preparation for receiving Communion, especially if you have not been to confession recently and are aware of having committed venial sins.

VI. What if You Forget the Words

This is the most common worry of returning Catholics, and it should not be. Priests have heard every possible version of this prayer, including no version at all. If you forget the formula during confession, three things are true.

First, the sacrament is valid as long as you have contrition, the interior sorrow. The prayer expresses the sorrow; it does not create it. Second, you may pray the Act of Contrition in your own words. Simply tell God you are sorry for your sins and intend, with His grace, to amend your life. The priest will accept this without comment. Third, if you ask, the priest will often lead you through the prayer line by line. Saying "I'm sorry, Father, I don't remember the prayer" is a perfectly normal opening.

VII. The Short Act of Contrition

Brief Form (For Children and Quick Use)
O my God, I am sorry for my sins. I love You with all my heart. I am sorry I have hurt You. Help me to live like Jesus and not sin again. Amen.

This form is used in children's first confession preparation and is also a serviceable form for adults in moments when the longer prayer is not on hand. The interior movement — sorrow, love, intention — is what matters.

VIII. The Place of the Prayer in Confession

The ordinary structure of the Sacrament of Reconciliation looks like this:

  • Greeting — the priest welcomes you, makes the Sign of the Cross.
  • Reading or invitation — sometimes the priest will read a short Scripture passage.
  • Confession of sins — you name your sins, by kind and (for grave matter) by number, since your last confession.
  • Counsel and penance — the priest may offer brief guidance and assigns you a penance.
  • Act of Contrition — you pray the prayer, in any of its forms.
  • Absolution — the priest pronounces the words of absolution; God forgives.
  • Dismissal — the priest dismisses you ("Go in peace") and you depart.

For a fuller walkthrough of the whole sacrament for those returning after years away, see our companion guide on how to go to confession after a long absence.

IX. Saying the Prayer From the Heart

Familiarity with any prayer is both a gift and a danger. The gift is that the words rise without effort; the danger is that they rise only with the lips. The traditional remedy is the practice of recollection: before praying, take a single breath, become aware that God is present, and then begin. Pray it slowly. Mean each line as it leaves you. If your attention wanders, return to the line you are on; do not start over. Distraction is not a sin. Returning is the prayer.

One useful private discipline is to pray the Act of Contrition each night and to pause, in turn, on whichever phrase the day has made vivid. On a day of harsh words: I am sorry for my sins with all my heart. On a day of failure to help: I have failed to do good. On a day of fatigue: with Your help. The prayer is not a single sentence repeated; it is a doorway that opens differently each time you stand in front of it.
