Spiritual Awakening: Stages, Symptoms, and a Practical Path

What it actually is, what the tradition says, and what to do next.

A spiritual awakening is not an Instagram caption. It is one of the oldest and best-documented experiences in the human record — and the tradition that knows it best has been describing it carefully for two thousand years. This guide gives you the three classical stages, the actual physical and emotional symptoms, the signs that something has shifted, and a sober practical path that does not depend on a retreat in Bali.

I. What a Spiritual Awakening Actually Is

A spiritual awakening is a re-ordering of attention. The center of gravity of a life shifts — usually slowly, sometimes suddenly — from the surface of things to what lies beneath them. What previously consumed you stops mattering as much. What previously sat unnoticed begins to glow. This is not a feeling. It is a quiet structural change in how you see, and it is permanent in proportion to how deep it goes.

The Christian mystical tradition has the longest and most carefully tested vocabulary for this process. Other traditions (Sufi, Zen, Vedantic) describe overlapping territory in their own terms. What follows draws primarily from the Catholic mystical canon — John of the Cross, Teresa of Ávila, Bernard of Clairvaux, Thomas Merton — because that is the tradition most thoroughly mapped in English and because its categories cross over cleanly to readers from other backgrounds.

II. The Three Classical Stages

The mystics describe three stages, and the sequence has held up remarkably well across centuries of independent witness.

Stage One: The Purgative Way

The first stage is a slow cleaning out. Habits, attachments, self-deceptions, addictions, and small daily lies begin to feel intolerable. You become aware of them without being able to fully stop them. There is a great deal of effort and not much obvious progress. This stage looks externally like discipline; internally it often feels like failure. Most people quit here, either by abandoning the path or by manufacturing a fake arrival.

Stage Two: The Illuminative Way

The second stage begins when the cleaning starts working from a different angle. Instead of fighting habits, you find yourself losing interest in them. Prayer becomes simpler. Reading becomes deeper. The world becomes more vivid. There is often a stretch of what the tradition calls consolations — moments of unmistakable presence, peace, or felt understanding. These are gifts; they are not the goal. Many practitioners get stuck here by trying to manufacture or hold onto the consolations.

Stage Three: The Unitive Way

The third stage is what the tradition calls union — a steady, undramatic recognition that you and God are not two separate parties in negotiation but one act of being, with you as the small dependent term in the relationship. The hallmark of the unitive way is paradoxically that it stops being interesting. The fireworks of the illuminative stage have gone out. What remains is steady, simple, and unremarkable from the outside. The saints describe it as the only place from which real love of others becomes possible.

III. The Dark Night

Between stages two and three sits the most-discussed and least-understood feature of the path: the dark night of the soul, named by John of the Cross. The dark night is not depression. It is not crisis. It is the experience of having outgrown the felt presence of God that sustained the earlier stages, and finding that the deeper presence has not yet become available to consciousness. The lights have gone out and the eyes have not yet adjusted.

The classical advice during a dark night is consistent across teachers: keep the practices, do not seek to manufacture the feelings, do not assume you have done something wrong. The night is not punishment. It is the necessary clearing of the last attachments to one's own spiritual experience, which is itself a subtle form of self-love.

IV. The Symptoms (Physical and Emotional)

Searches for spiritual awakening symptoms and the 10 physical symptoms of spiritual awakening turn up lists that range from useful to absurd. The honest version, drawn from the tradition rather than from wellness blogs, looks like this.

  • Sleep disturbances — both insomnia and unusual vividness of dreams. The interior life intensifies; the unconscious works harder.
  • Changes in appetite — both directions are common. Eating less is more typical in early stages.
  • Sensitivity to noise, crowds, and screens — what previously did not register now exhausts.
  • Tears at unexpected moments — including during prayer, music, or quiet conversation. The tradition calls this the gift of tears and treats it with reverence.
  • Loss of interest in things that previously consumed you — entertainments, status games, certain friendships, certain ambitions.
  • Increased interest in things you previously dismissed — silence, walking, reading, prayer, ordinary work, the company of children.
  • Emotional volatility — particularly in early purgative stages. Old wounds surface for processing.
  • Synchronicity — a heightened awareness of patterns and meaningful coincidences. The tradition is cautious here; this is real but easily over-interpreted.
  • Energy shifts — periods of unusual stamina alternating with periods of deep fatigue.
  • A felt sense of being watched, accompanied, or held — often subtle, never grandiose. If it is grandiose, it is probably not the genuine article.

V. The Signs

Searches for signs of chosen one by god or chosen ones spiritual awakening point at a real question that the tradition answers carefully. The Christian view is that every person is called and every person is loved, individually and completely. The question is not whether you are chosen but whether you are listening. The signs that you are beginning to listen are the symptoms above, plus three deeper indicators.

  1. A growing inability to lie to yourself about your own motives. The interior camera has been switched on.
  2. A growing willingness to be inconvenienced for the sake of someone else, including someone who has not earned it.
  3. A growing capacity to remain still in situations that would previously have required a reaction.

These three are not feelings. They are observable changes in behavior. If a "spiritual awakening" leaves a person more easily offended, more performatively certain, or more inclined to teach than to listen, the awakening has not yet matured into anything serious.

VI. Scriptures for Revival and Spiritual Awakening

The Bible is the deepest record of awakening that the Christian tradition holds. A few passages are read across the centuries as particularly direct on the subject.

  • Isaiah 43:18-19 — "Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?"
  • Ezekiel 36:26 — "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh."
  • Romans 12:2 — "Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind."
  • Psalm 51:10 — "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me."
  • Ephesians 5:14 — "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you."
  • Revelation 3:20 — "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him."

VII. A Practical Path

The path is not complicated. It is just hard. Five practices, in order of difficulty.

  1. Daily silence — twenty minutes, same time, same place. No phone. No agenda. This is the foundation. Without it, nothing else holds.
  2. A short daily reading — Scripture, a spiritual classic, or a contemporary work in the lineage. Ten minutes. The same passage if needed for weeks.
  3. A weekly examination of conscience — fifteen minutes on a Sunday. What rose, what fell, what was true, what was avoided.
  4. A weekly act of service that costs you something — not money, time and attention. The kind of help no one will thank you for.
  5. The sacraments, if you are a Catholic returning — confession monthly, Communion weekly. The tradition is unanimous that the inner life does not flourish without the sacramental life. (See our guides on the act of contrition and how to go to confession for the practical mechanics.)

Most people fail at the first one for the first month. This is normal. Do it badly until you can do it well. The tradition is also unanimous that no one has ever been worse off for spending twenty minutes a day in silence.

VIII. Where the Path Leads

The destination is not a state. It is a person — both yours and God's. The mystics describe the unitive stage in terms not of arriving anywhere but of becoming someone whose presence is itself a kind of prayer. The reading life that supports this work is documented in the companion guide on spiritual awakening books, and the longer literary expression of the whole path is in Santiago Vitagliano's The Journey Begins Within.
