How to Become Catholic: A Practical Guide to RCIA

The four stages, the timeline, and what to expect from beginning to first Communion.

If you have been considering becoming Catholic — whether you grew up in another tradition, were baptized but never instructed, or were raised with no faith at all — the path is more open and more orderly than most people expect. This guide walks you through the entire process: the four stages of the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults, the realistic timeline, what to expect at each step, and how to choose the parish community that will carry you through.

I. The Short Answer

To become Catholic as an adult, you enter a program called OCIA — the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults (formerly called RCIA, the Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults; many parishes still use the older name). The program runs at most parishes from late summer through the following Easter, lasts about nine months, and culminates in your reception into the Catholic faith at the Easter Vigil Mass. The whole thing is free. The first step is to call or email the parish nearest you and ask to speak with the OCIA director.

That is the short answer. What follows is the longer one, because the short version makes the process sound more mechanical than it is. OCIA is not a class. It is a guided journey, and the parts that change you most are the parts not printed on the calendar.

II. Who OCIA Is For

The program is structured to receive three categories of adults, and the same group typically includes all three.

  • The unbaptized — adults who were never baptized in any Christian tradition. They are called catechumens. They will be baptized, confirmed, and receive First Communion at the Easter Vigil.
  • Baptized non-Catholic Christians — adults baptized in another Christian denomination (Protestant, Orthodox, etc.) who wish to become Catholic. They are called candidates. They are not re-baptized (the Catholic faith recognizes valid baptism in other traditions) but they make a profession of faith, receive Confirmation, and receive First Communion.
  • Baptized Catholics never catechized — adults baptized Catholic as infants but never confirmed and never instructed in the faith. They typically prepare for Confirmation and First Communion alongside the catechumens and candidates.

The journey looks somewhat different for each category, but most of the formation runs together because the material — the faith itself — is the same.

III. The Four Stages

The Order of Christian Initiation moves through four formally distinct stages. The pacing is intentional. Each stage has its own rhythm and purpose.

Stage 1: The Period of Inquiry (Pre-Catechumenate)

This is the open-door stage. Usually June through August. You attend informal meetings, ask questions, listen, and discover whether the Catholic faith is something you want to pursue further. No commitment is required. Many parishes treat this stage as ongoing — you can join at almost any time. The point is to learn what you would be saying yes to before you say it.

What it feels like: a conversation, not a course. The OCIA team is trained to listen more than to teach at this stage.

Stage 2: The Catechumenate

This is the main instructional stage and the longest. Usually September through Lent. Weekly meetings cover the Creed, the sacraments, the moral life, prayer, Scripture, and Catholic history. You attend Mass each Sunday (the catechumens are formally dismissed after the homily for further reflection; candidates often stay). You are matched with a sponsor — a confirmed Catholic who walks alongside you and supports your journey.

What it feels like: a slow build. Most people experience their deepest questions and their deepest doubts here. Both are welcome.

Stage 3: The Period of Purification and Enlightenment

This is Lent — the forty days before Easter. The focus shifts from learning to preparing. You participate in the Rite of Election (usually at the cathedral with the bishop, on the First Sunday of Lent) and in three scrutinies during Lent. Prayer intensifies. Reading slows. The community prays for you specifically by name at Sunday Mass.

What it feels like: something is being asked of you that is bigger than the curriculum. This is intentional.

Stage 4: The Sacraments of Initiation and Mystagogy

At the Easter Vigil Mass on Holy Saturday night, the catechumens are baptized, confirmed, and receive First Communion. Candidates make their profession of faith and are confirmed and receive First Communion. The Mystagogy period that follows (Easter through Pentecost) is a kind of debriefing: weekly meetings to reflect on the experience of the sacraments and to begin living out the Catholic faith as a full member of the body of believers.

What it feels like: a beginning, not an ending.

IV. The Realistic Timeline

If you reach out to a parish in June or July, you can typically be received at the following Easter Vigil, a span of nine to ten months. If you reach out later in the year, you may still be welcomed into an ongoing group, but full reception sometimes shifts a year. The pacing is deliberately slow; you are not being delayed, you are being formed.

It is also possible — and increasingly common — for OCIA to be tailored individually rather than run as a single annual cohort. If you have specific circumstances (you are moving, you are getting married, you are dying), most parishes will work with you. Call and ask.

V. What You Will Be Asked

At various points in the process, you will be asked questions. The honest version of each is below.

  • Why do you want to become Catholic? Almost any honest answer is acceptable. "I am drawn to the Eucharist." "I have read my way here." "My spouse is Catholic and I have come to want what they have." "I do not yet know, but I want to find out."
  • Do you accept what the Catholic faith teaches? The expected answer is yes, with the understanding that you are still learning. You do not need to fully understand every doctrine before being received; you need to be willing to accept the teaching of the Catholic faith and continue to grow in understanding.
  • Will you live the moral life that the faith asks of you? The most practically important question. It includes serious matters: the indissolubility of marriage, sexual ethics, regular participation in the sacraments. If there are obstacles in your life (a prior marriage, a current cohabitation, etc.), the OCIA team will help you navigate them. Most situations have a path forward.

VI. How to Choose a Parish

You can become Catholic at any Catholic parish, but the parish you choose will shape the experience considerably. A few practical notes.

  • Geography first. The parish closest to where you live is usually the right starting point because that is where you will be a member after reception. Drive ten miles to a "better" parish and you will burn out within two years.
  • Visit a Sunday Mass before you call. Watch the community. The Catholic faith is universal, but parishes have personalities. Some are quiet and traditional; some are warm and contemporary. You will know within one or two visits whether you can pray with these people for the long haul.
  • Talk to two OCIA directors if your area has multiple parishes. The director sets the tone of the whole program. A director who actually loves the work is worth a longer drive.

VII. What to Read While You Wait

A short reading list to keep you company through the inquiry stage.

  • The Catechism of the Catholic Faith — the official compendium. Not light reading; do not try to read it cover to cover. Use the index.
  • YOUCAT — the youth-and-adult catechism in question-and-answer form. Excellent first orientation.
  • Mere Christianity by C.S. Lewis — the most-recommended apologetic of the twentieth century. Lewis was Anglican, not Catholic, but the book is foundational across traditions.
  • Rome Sweet Home by Scott and Kimberly Hahn — the conversion story most often cited by adult converts as the one that opened the door.
  • The Confessions by Saint Augustine — the original spiritual autobiography. The first book about an inner life ever written. Still the deepest.
  • The Journey Begins Within by Santiago Vitagliano — a contemporary literary entry on the inner work that any serious religious life requires. Read more.

VIII. The First Step

Find the website of the parish nearest you. Look for "OCIA" or "RCIA" or "becoming Catholic" or "adult faith formation." Email or call. Most parishes respond within a few days. If they do not, call the next parish. The doors really do open in.

For those returning to the practice after years away rather than entering from outside, the same parish office can connect you with the sacrament of Reconciliation and a gentle re-entry. See our companion guides on how to go to confession and the act of contrition.
