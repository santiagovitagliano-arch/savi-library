Spiritual Awakening Stages: The Three Classical Phases

Purgative, illuminative, unitive — and the dark night between them.

The Christian mystical tradition maps the spiritual life in three classical stages: the purgative way, the illuminative way, and the unitive way. The framework comes from the early monastic teachers, was refined by John of the Cross and Teresa of Ávila, and remains the most thoroughly tested description of inner growth in any tradition. This guide walks through each stage, names what it feels like, and tells you how long to expect each to last.

Why Stages At All

The honest answer is that the inner life has phases the way the body has growth phases. The work of an inner toddler is not the work of an inner adolescent. The work of an inner adolescent is not the work of an inner adult. Treating every stage as if it were the same — by demanding constant spiritual highs, or by getting stuck in early disciplines, or by skipping the foundational work — is what produces most of the spiritual confusion you see around you.

The three stages overlap, recur, and circle back at deeper levels. The framework is not a ladder; it is a map. You may be in the purgative phase on one issue and the illuminative on another at the same time.

The Three Stages

Stage One: The Purgative Way

Also called: the way of beginners · Typical duration: months to years · Hardest stage

The first stage is a slow cleaning out. Habits, attachments, self-deceptions, addictions, and small daily lies begin to feel intolerable. You become aware of them without being able to fully stop them. The interior camera has been switched on, and what it shows is not flattering.

What it feels like: effort and frustration, often simultaneous. You are trying. You are failing. You are trying again. You begin a daily prayer, miss days, restart, miss more. You catch yourself in old patterns and feel both more aware and less able to change. Externally, this stage looks like discipline. Internally, it usually feels like failure.

The temptation: to quit. Either by abandoning the path or by manufacturing a fake arrival — declaring yourself "spiritual enough" and stopping the actual work. Most people drop out here.

The right move: keep going. The frustration is not a sign you are doing it wrong; it is a sign you are doing it right. The classical teachers are unanimous that this stage cannot be skipped or shortened by force of will — only by persistence.

Stage Two: The Illuminative Way

Also called: the way of proficients · Typical duration: years to decades · Sweet stage

The second stage begins when the cleaning starts working from a different angle. Instead of fighting habits, you find yourself losing interest in them. Prayer becomes simpler. Reading becomes deeper. The world becomes more vivid. There is often a stretch of what the tradition calls consolations — moments of unmistakable peace, presence, felt understanding.

What it feels like: relief, followed by a long sweetness, followed eventually by a slow draining of the sweetness. The early illuminative stage is the most pleasant phase of the journey; the late illuminative is when things get harder again.

The temptation: to try to manufacture or hold onto the consolations. To become attached to your own spiritual experiences. To make the felt sweetness the point.

The right move: receive the gifts gratefully without grasping. Continue the practices that got you here. Notice that you are being shaped, not entertained.

The Dark Night of the Soul

Between Stages Two and Three · Typical duration: months to years · Most misunderstood

Between the illuminative and unitive stages sits the most-discussed and least-understood feature of the path: the dark night, named by Saint John of the Cross. The dark night is not depression. It is not crisis. It is the experience of having outgrown the felt presence of God that sustained the earlier stages, and finding that the deeper presence has not yet become available to consciousness.

What it feels like: the lights have gone out. Prayer feels empty. Reading does not land. The practices that previously fed you now seem dry, mechanical, pointless. You may wonder if you have lost the faith.

The temptation: to abandon the practices because they no longer "work." To seek consolation in lower goods. To assume you have failed.

The right move: the classical advice is consistent across teachers — keep the practices, do not seek to manufacture the feelings, do not assume you have done something wrong. The night is not punishment. It is the necessary clearing of the last attachments to one's own spiritual experience.

Stage Three: The Unitive Way

Also called: the way of the perfect · Typical duration: the rest of one's life · Surprising stage

The third stage is what the tradition calls union — a steady, undramatic recognition that you and God are not two separate parties in negotiation but one act of being, with you as the small dependent term in the relationship.

What it feels like: ordinary. The hallmark of the unitive way is paradoxically that it stops being interesting. The fireworks of the illuminative stage have gone out. What remains is steady, simple, and unremarkable from the outside. The saints describe it as the only place from which real love of others becomes possible — your attention has finally been freed from its own interior.

The temptation: to think you have arrived. To stop the practices. To assume the work is done.

The right move: continue. The unitive way is not a destination but a steady state that can deepen indefinitely. The greatest saints describe themselves at the end of their lives as still beginners.

What the Masters Say

  • Bernard of Clairvaux taught four degrees of love: love of self for self's sake; love of God for self's sake; love of God for God's sake; love of self for God's sake. Each corresponds roughly to a stage.
  • Teresa of Ávila wrote of seven mansions in the interior castle; the first three roughly correspond to the purgative way, the next three to the illuminative, and the seventh to the unitive.
  • John of the Cross mapped the territory in Ascent of Mount Carmel and Dark Night of the Soul with more precision than anyone before or since. If you read only one teacher on this subject, read him.
  • Thomas Merton brought the framework into the twentieth century in New Seeds of Contemplation.

How to Know Where You Are

You usually do not. The classical wisdom is that asking which stage you are in is itself a sign of the earlier stages. The signs are these: The purgative is marked by effort. The illuminative is marked by sweetness. The dark night is marked by dryness. The unitive is marked by ordinariness. Most readers, asking honestly, recognize themselves in the first or second.
