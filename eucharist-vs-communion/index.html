Eucharist vs Communion: The Real Difference

The Catholic teaching on the Real Presence, the Protestant alternatives, and why the distinction matters.

"Eucharist" and "Communion" are sometimes used interchangeably, but they name different things. The Catholic understanding is that the Eucharist is Christ — body, blood, soul, and divinity — under the appearances of bread and wine. Most Protestant traditions teach something different. This guide walks through the distinction carefully, names the historical positions, and explains why this is the question at the center of Catholic life.

I. The Terms

Eucharist comes from the Greek eucharistia, meaning "thanksgiving." It is the word the early Christians used for the sacrament Jesus instituted at the Last Supper, and the term the Catholic faith still uses for both the sacrament itself and the consecrated species (the host and the chalice). When a Catholic says "the Eucharist," she means the Real Presence of Christ.

Communion is a broader and more variable term. In Catholic usage, "receiving Communion" or "Holy Communion" refers to the act of receiving the Eucharist. In Protestant usage, "Communion" can refer to the same act but with a different theology behind it — often a memorial, a symbolic remembering, rather than a real presence. The same word names different things in different traditions.

II. The Catholic Teaching: The Real Presence

The Catholic faith teaches that at the consecration of the Mass, the bread and wine become — substantially, really, truly — the body and blood of Christ. The appearances (taste, look, weight, chemistry) remain unchanged; the substance is changed. This is what the Council of Trent named transubstantiation.

The teaching comes from Christ's own words at the Last Supper:

"Take, eat; this is my body... Drink of it, all of you, for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins."
— Matthew 26:26-28

And from John 6, where Jesus speaks plainly enough that many of his disciples leave:

"Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you... For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink."
— John 6:53, 55

When his hearers protest, Jesus does not soften the teaching. He repeats it. Many leave him over it (John 6:66). The Catholic position is that the literal reading was the original reading, and that the unbroken tradition of the early body of believers — including writers like Ignatius of Antioch (107 AD), Justin Martyr (155 AD), and Augustine — confirms it.

III. The Protestant Alternatives

The Reformation produced several different theologies of the Lord's Supper, each diverging from the Catholic position in a distinct way.

TraditionPosition
Catholic / OrthodoxReal Presence. The bread and wine are substantially changed into the body and blood of Christ.
LutheranSacramental Union. Christ is truly present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine, but without transubstantiation. Sometimes called consubstantiation, though Lutherans dislike the term.
Anglican (high)Real Presence (varied). Many high-church Anglicans hold a position close to the Catholic teaching; others hold a more spiritual or receptionist view.
Reformed (Calvinist)Spiritual Presence. Christ is truly present spiritually to the faithful believer who receives by faith, but the bread and wine themselves are not changed.
Baptist / EvangelicalMemorial View (Zwinglian). The bread and wine are symbols of Christ's body and blood. The act is one of remembrance, not real reception.

The Catholic teaching is the historically older position. The memorial view associated with Zwingli was a sixteenth-century innovation. The Lutheran and Reformed positions sit between the two extremes.

IV. Why the Distinction Matters

If the Eucharist is what the Catholic faith teaches it is — Christ himself — then the Mass is the single most important thing that happens on earth. It is not a community gathering with a symbolic meal at the center. It is the same sacrifice of Calvary made present in time, with the same Christ offering the same self under the species of bread and wine. The Catholic who genuflects before the tabernacle is not bowing toward a metaphor.

If the Eucharist is a memorial symbol, then Sunday worship is essentially educational and communal, with the bread and wine as didactic props. Both are valuable, but they are different things, and the difference shapes the entire rhythm of religious life.

This is why the Catholic faith has never permitted intercommunion with Protestant traditions that do not share the doctrine of the Real Presence. It is not exclusion for its own sake. It is the position that pretending agreement on the most important point would be a worse offense than the disagreement itself.

V. The Eucharist Meaning and Significance for Daily Life

For a practicing Catholic, the Eucharist is not a weekly event among other events. It is the center to which the rest of the week orients. Daily Mass, where available, is the highest privilege a layperson has access to. Eucharistic adoration — time spent in the presence of the consecrated host exposed in a monstrance — is the contemplative discipline most directly aligned with Catholic sacramental theology.

If you have not received Communion in some time and want to return, the path is straightforward. Go to confession first (see our companion guides on how to go to confession and the act of contrition) if you are aware of grave sin. Attend Mass. Receive. The first reception after a long absence is often the most powerful one most Catholics ever have.
