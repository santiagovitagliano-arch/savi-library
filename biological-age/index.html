Biological Age: What It Is, How to Test It, and How to Lower It

The number that matters more than the one on your driver's license.

Chronological age counts years since birth. Biological age estimates how much wear, damage, and decline your body has actually accumulated. Two people of the same chronological age can have biological ages a decade apart — and the gap is the lever you can actually move. This guide explains what biological age is, the major tests available, what changes the score, and how to lower it.

I. Biological Age vs Chronological Age

Your chronological age is a count of revolutions of the earth around the sun since you were born. It is a useful administrative fact and a poor predictor of your health, function, or life expectancy. A sixty-five-year-old who hikes, sleeps eight hours, lifts weights, and has a robust social life is biologically a different animal from a sixty-five-year-old who is sedentary, lonely, sleep-deprived, and metabolically inflamed. The driver's license says they are the same age. The medical reality says they are not.

Biological age estimates the second number. It is calculated from biomarkers that change with cumulative damage — DNA methylation patterns, inflammatory markers, metabolic indicators, organ-system function. The estimates are not perfect, but they correlate with all-cause mortality risk more strongly than chronological age does. That is what makes them useful.

II. The Major Tests

Horvath Clock (2013)

The original epigenetic clock, developed by Steve Horvath at UCLA. Measures DNA methylation at 353 specific sites and outputs an age estimate. Works across tissues. Best for research; less actionable for individuals because it correlates more with chronological age than with mortality risk specifically.

PhenoAge (2018)

Developed by Morgan Levine. Trained directly on clinical biomarkers associated with mortality. More predictive of disease and mortality than Horvath. Available from several commercial labs.

GrimAge (2019)

Currently the most predictive of all-cause mortality of any widely-available test. Trained on plasma proteins linked to lifespan. Available through TruDiagnostic and a small number of other labs. Expensive but the gold standard.

DunedinPACE (2022)

Different in concept: rather than estimating biological age at a moment, it estimates the rate at which you are biologically aging right now (pace of aging per chronological year). A score of 1.0 means you are aging at the average rate. Below 1.0 is slower; above 1.0 is faster. Particularly responsive to intervention; useful for tracking changes.

OMICmAge / Index of Biological Age

Newer multi-omic indices combining methylation, proteins, and metabolomics. Most accurate but most expensive and least available.

III. What Actually Changes the Score

The interventions with the largest documented effects on biological age, ranked by effect size.

  1. Smoking cessation. Largest single intervention. GrimAge in particular responds strongly. If you smoke, this is the lever.
  2. Cardiovascular fitness. VO2 max improvements are associated with measurable reductions in biological age across multiple clocks.
  3. Resistance training. Muscle mass and grip strength are independently associated with biological-age benefits.
  4. Sleep quality. Both duration and consistency. Sleep deprivation accelerates GrimAge.
  5. Whole-food nutrition. Higher fiber, more polyphenols, less ultra-processed food. The CALERIE trial showed mild caloric restriction slowed DunedinPACE by ~2% over two years.
  6. Stress and inflammation. Chronic inflammation (hsCRP elevated) is among the strongest biological-age accelerators.
  7. Social connection. Less directly studied but emerging evidence is strong; loneliness shows up in biological age markers.

IV. A Practical Test-and-Iterate Plan

  1. Baseline test. Pick one of the better-validated clocks (GrimAge or DunedinPACE if budget allows; PhenoAge if not). Cost: roughly $200-$500.
  2. Implement the five pillars for six to twelve months. Sleep, resistance training, cardiovascular fitness, whole-food nutrition, community. (See our companion article on longevity for the full pillar framework.)
  3. Retest at twelve months. The DunedinPACE clock in particular tends to show the clearest changes in this window.
  4. Iterate. If the number moved, double down on what worked. If not, examine adherence honestly. Most "failed interventions" are failed implementations.

Free calculators (PhenoAge can be computed from a standard blood panel) are an acceptable starting point if a commercial test is out of budget. See our companion guide on the biological age calculator options.

V. Common Mistakes

  • Testing too often. Most clocks move slowly. Annual is the right cadence; quarterly is noise.
  • Chasing the score. The number is a proxy. Optimizing for the score while ignoring how you feel and function is the wrong frame.
  • Stacking interventions and not knowing what worked. Change one major variable at a time when iterating; otherwise you cannot attribute results.
  • Reading the absolute number too literally. The error bars on most tests are 2-4 years. Trends matter more than single readings.

VI. The Bigger Picture

Biological age is a tool, not a goal. The goal is a long, capable, meaningful life — measured in good function, real relationships, and work worth doing. The number is a useful summary of the substrate that supports all of that. Lower is better, all else equal. But the deeper longevity question is not how do I lower this number; it is what kind of life am I building, and will the years be worth living.
