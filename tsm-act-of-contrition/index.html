The Act of Contrition: The Catholic Prayer That Opens the Door to Confession

The full text, the meaning behind every line, and how the Act of Contrition fits inside the larger sacramental life of the Catholic faith. Trilingual: English, Spanish, Portuguese.

Most people who search for the Act of Contrition are not looking for theology. They are looking for the prayer itself, in the moment they need it: kneeling in a confessional, sitting beside a hospital bed, lying awake at the end of a difficult day. The prayer is short. It can be memorized in an afternoon. But the line that runs through it — the recognition that sorrow for sin opens the path back to communion with God — is the same line that runs through the whole Catholic faith.

This page gives you the prayer in three languages, explains what each part actually means, and shows how the Act of Contrition connects to confession, to the Eucharist, and to the larger journey that brings people into or back to the Catholic faith. For longer doctrinal reflections, see the reflections library at The SAVI Ministries and the institutional publications page.

What the Act of Contrition Is

The Act of Contrition is a prayer of sorrow for sin. It is recited at the end of sacramental confession, before the priest's absolution, and it can also be prayed privately — at night, during an examination of conscience, or whenever the soul becomes aware of the weight of sin. Several versions exist; the Church has never required a single fixed wording. What it requires is the substance: an honest acknowledgement of wrongdoing, sorrow for that wrong, a firm purpose of amendment, and trust in God's mercy.

The traditional English text

O my God, I am heartily sorry for having offended Thee, and I detest all my sins because of Thy just punishments, but most of all because they offend Thee, my God, who art all-good and deserving of all my love. I firmly resolve, with the help of Thy grace, to confess my sins, to do penance, and to amend my life. Amen.

The contemporary English text

My God, I am sorry for my sins with all my heart. In choosing to do wrong and failing to do good, I have sinned against you whom I should love above all things. I firmly intend, with your help, to do penance, to sin no more, and to avoid whatever leads me to sin. Our Savior Jesus Christ suffered and died for us. In his name, my God, have mercy. Amen.

Both texts say the same thing. Pick the one that fits your parish, your formation, and your interior register. A reverent old translation is not better than a clear contemporary one, and the reverse is also true.

What Each Part of the Prayer Actually Means

The Act of Contrition is short because its theology is dense. Each clause carries a doctrinal weight that the Church teaches at length elsewhere — in the Catechism, in the writings of the saints, and in the lived practice of confession.

"I am heartily sorry"

This is the act of contrition proper. The Church distinguishes between two kinds of sorrow for sin. Perfect contrition is sorrow that flows from the love of God, grieving sin because it offends the One who is loved. Imperfect contrition — also called attrition — is sorrow that flows from the fear of punishment or from awareness of the ugliness of sin. Both are valid. Both lead to confession. The Act of Contrition reaches for the higher kind, even when the heart is only able to offer the lower kind in the moment.

"I detest all my sins"

This is the renunciation. Detesting sin is not the same as feeling bad. It is the decision of the will to be done with what has been wrong. This is also the line that connects the prayer to the practical question many Catholics search for: what should I actually say at confession? The answer is straightforward. Examples of sins to say at confession include the specific actions — by category, by approximate frequency, and (for mortal sin) by kind — that the examination of conscience has surfaced. A thorough trilingual examination is taught inside the first module of the Inner Chamber seminar. The prayer of contrition does not replace that work; it crowns it.

"I firmly resolve, with the help of Thy grace"

This is the purpose of amendment. It is not a promise to be perfect. It is a promise to try, with the help of grace, to not return to the same sin. Catholic moral theology has never required a guarantee of future perfection — only an honest intent in the present moment.

"To confess my sins, to do penance, and to amend my life"

This is the bridge into the sacrament. The Act of Contrition expects that confession will follow, that the priest will assign a penance, and that the life of the penitent will look different on the other side of the confessional door.

How the Act of Contrition Fits Into the Sacrament of Confession

For Catholics who haven't been to confession in years — or who have never been — the practical question is bigger than the prayer. The good news is that the structure of the sacrament has not changed. If you are asking how to go to confession, the steps are these:

  • Examine your conscience. Quietly, honestly, before you go. A simple framework: walk through the Ten Commandments, the precepts of the Church, and the duties of your state in life.
  • Enter the confessional. Greet the priest. Make the sign of the cross. Say: "Bless me, Father, for I have sinned. It has been [X amount of time] since my last confession."
  • Confess your sins. Be specific enough that the priest understands; do not embellish. Mortal sins must be confessed by kind and approximate number. Venial sins are encouraged but not required.
  • Listen. The priest may offer counsel. He will assign a penance.
  • Pray the Act of Contrition. Now is when this prayer goes to work.
  • Receive absolution. The priest says the words of forgiveness; you respond Amen.
  • Leave. Do your penance. Begin again.

For a more thorough preparation, many parishes and ministries publish a downloadable Catholic confession guide PDF with a full examination of conscience. The Theology Behind the Prayer

Catholic apologetics — the defense of the faith — has a long tradition of explaining why sacramental confession exists at all. The short answer comes from Christ Himself, who said to the apostles: "Receive the Holy Spirit. Whose sins you forgive are forgiven them, and whose sins you retain are retained" (John 20:22–23). The Catholic Church understands this as the institution of the sacrament of reconciliation. The priest is not the source of forgiveness; God is. The priest is the minister Christ chose to convey it.

The Act of Contrition is the moment in the sacrament when the penitent makes interior what the rite makes exterior. The words of absolution are spoken by the priest; the sorrow is spoken by the penitent. Together they enact the meeting of mercy and repentance that the prayer describes.

For Those Returning to the Faith — or Becoming Catholic

A real portion of the people who type "act of contrition" into a search bar are not lifelong Catholics. They are people who left the faith years ago and are quietly wondering how to come back. They are also people who never were Catholic and are asking how to become Catholic — sometimes how to become Catholic as an adult, sometimes online, sometimes in their own city without anyone they know finding out.

The path back is straightforward. For a baptized Catholic who has been away, it begins with a single confession — sometimes called a general confession, covering the years since the last one. For someone entering the faith for the first time, it begins with the process the Church calls OCIA (formerly RCIA): a period of formation, catechesis, and discernment that culminates at the Easter Vigil with baptism, confirmation, and first Eucharist. The Act of Contrition will be one of the first prayers learned along that path.

The companion text most often used inside The SAVI Ministries formation track is The Journey Begins Within by Santiago Vitagliano — a book written specifically for readers who are returning to the faith, considering the Catholic Church for the first time, or recovering an inner-life practice that was lost in adulthood. The Eucharist Is What the Act of Contrition Is Pointing Toward

Confession is not the end of the sacramental life. It is the doorway. The eucharist meaning and significance is what stands on the other side of that door. The difference between Eucharist vs. communion is mostly a matter of language: in Catholic teaching the Eucharist is the sacrament, and Holy Communion is the act of receiving it. The Church teaches that the bread and wine, after consecration, are no longer bread and wine — they are the body and blood of Christ, really and substantially present. This is what is meant by the doctrine of the real presence.

The Act of Contrition prepares the soul for that encounter. A Catholic in a state of mortal sin should not receive the Eucharist until they have been to confession. A Catholic in a state of grace receives the Eucharist as the food of the journey. The prayer of contrition is, in this sense, the threshold between the two. For a deeper treatment of real presence and the moral preconditions for receiving Communion, see the relevant messages and reflections at thesaviministries.org.

How to Pray the Rosary Alongside the Act of Contrition

Many Catholics fold the Act of Contrition into the daily Rosary. The structure of the Rosary — the five decades, the five mysteries, the Our Father and Hail Mary and Glory Be — is the inheritance of centuries. The 5 mysteries of the Rosary, prayed in rotation by day, walk the soul through the life of Christ as seen through the eyes of His mother. The Act of Contrition, prayed at the beginning or end, places the whole exercise in the right interior posture. The Inner Chamber seminar treats the Rosary as a contemplative scaffold — see module one for the framing.

Those who want to pray the Rosary in Spanish — Cómo rezar el Rosario — will find the structure identical. The Hail Mary becomes the Ave Maria. The Our Father becomes the Padre Nuestro. The mysteries are the same mysteries. Downloadable versions in PDF are widely available, including from the Spanish-language Salud module preview and from Catholic publishers serving the Latin American faithful.

A Note on Mercy

The Act of Contrition is not a prayer of self-loathing. It is a prayer of trust. The line that says "I detest all my sins because they offend Thee, my God, who art all-good and deserving of all my love" is not the language of someone who hates themselves. It is the language of someone who has come to understand, finally, that the only proportionate response to having wounded a love of that magnitude is sorrow. And that the sorrow, once spoken, is met with the mercy that has been waiting the whole time.

This is why the prayer ends as it does — not in despair but in resolution. "I firmly resolve, with the help of Thy grace." The grace is already on offer. The prayer is the soul saying yes to it.
