Longevity Supplements: An Honest Evaluation Framework

What the evidence actually says, what to ignore, and what to do instead.

The longevity supplement market is worth tens of billions of dollars and is almost entirely unregulated. The science behind some compounds is real and interesting. The marketing behind almost all of them is not. This page gives you the framework we teach at the Health Protocol Seminar: three questions to ask before buying anything, a candid evaluation of the major compounds, and a clear answer to what the best longevity supplements actually are for most adults.

This article is general health education, not medical advice. Discuss any supplement decision with a physician who knows your full health history. Supplements can interact with medications and conditions in ways that this page cannot anticipate.

I. The Three Questions

Before evaluating any specific compound, anchor yourself to three questions. Most longevity supplements fail at least one of them. The ones that pass all three are the ones worth considering.

Question 1: Biological Plausibility

Does the proposed mechanism actually make sense? Which of the hallmarks of aging does it claim to address — cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, autophagy, nutrient sensing? A supplement marketed as "anti-aging" without a clear mechanistic story is selling vibes.

Question 2: Evidence Quality

What kind of evidence is there, and in what species? Mouse studies and human studies are not the same thing. Observational human studies and randomized controlled trials are not the same thing. The honest hierarchy: randomized controlled trials in humans > large prospective cohort studies in humans > small human trials > primate studies > rodent studies > cell culture studies > theoretical mechanism. Most "promising longevity supplements" sit in the bottom half of that list.

Question 3: Opportunity Cost

What would the same money, attention, and biological bandwidth buy if spent elsewhere? Two thousand dollars a year on rapamycin protocol vs. two thousand dollars a year on a personal trainer is not the same intervention. The supplements that fail this question fail it badly, because the five practical pillars of longevity (sleep, resistance training, cardiovascular fitness, nutrition, community) have larger effect sizes than any supplement currently on the market.

II. Evaluation of the Major Compounds

What follows is our current read of the most-discussed longevity supplements, scored against the three questions. The scores reflect human evidence quality and risk-adjusted upside as of 2026.

Rapamycin (Sirolimus) Mixed

An mTOR inhibitor with the strongest pre-clinical longevity data of any compound — extends lifespan in every model organism tested. Human evidence is early but suggestive of benefit on immune aging and possibly cardiovascular health at low intermittent doses. Significant side-effect profile, prescription required in most jurisdictions, ongoing research on dosing protocols. Currently best treated as an experimental intervention under medical supervision, not a casual addition.

Metformin Mixed

A first-line diabetes drug with a long safety record and observational data suggesting longevity benefits in diabetics. The TAME trial (Targeting Aging with Metformin) was designed to settle the question in non-diabetics; its results have been more equivocal than early advocates predicted. There is some evidence that metformin may blunt exercise adaptations in the muscle, which matters because exercise is a higher-leverage intervention than the drug. Currently a defensible choice for some metabolically vulnerable adults, not a default.

NAD Precursors — NMN and NR Mixed

Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) raise cellular NAD+ levels, which decline with age. Mouse studies are striking; human studies show NAD+ does rise but downstream clinical benefits remain unclear. The FDA reclassified NMN as a drug rather than a supplement in 2022, which has muddled US availability. Generally well-tolerated. Most likely useful for older adults with documented metabolic decline; less clearly useful for healthy younger adults.

Senolytics (Fisetin, Quercetin, Dasatinib) Mixed

The senolytic hypothesis — periodically clearing senescent "zombie" cells to reduce inflammation and tissue dysfunction — is one of the most intellectually exciting ideas in the field. Human evidence remains early. Fisetin in particular has good pre-clinical data and a generous safety profile. Episodic dosing (a few days every few weeks or months) is the typical protocol. Watch this space; the data is improving.

Urolithin A Mixed

A postbiotic metabolite produced by gut bacteria from pomegranates and walnuts (in people whose microbiomes can make it). Promotes mitophagy — the recycling of damaged mitochondria. Several reasonably designed human trials show improvements in muscle endurance and mitochondrial markers in older adults. Effect sizes are modest. Worth considering for adults over 50 working on cardiovascular fitness.

Taurine Mixed

An amino acid that declines with age. A widely-publicized 2023 paper in Science showed lifespan extension in mice and improved biomarkers in monkeys. Human trials are early. Cheap, well-tolerated, easy to add. Reasonable to consider; the evidence will firm up over the next few years.

Glycine + N-Acetylcysteine (GlyNAC) Mixed

The combination supports glutathione synthesis, the body's master antioxidant. Small human trials in older adults show improvements in multiple aging biomarkers (oxidative stress, insulin resistance, inflammation, mitochondrial function) over 16 weeks. Promising. Needs larger trials. The two compounds are cheap and individually well-studied.

Resveratrol Weak

The compound that launched a thousand longevity startups in the 2000s. Human evidence has largely failed to replicate the early enthusiasm. Bioavailability is poor. Probably not worth the money. The grapes and red wine it came from carry the same polyphenols at lower doses and with all of the other co-factors.

Vitamin D + K2 Strong

The most boring entry on this list and arguably the most important. Vitamin D deficiency is genuinely common, genuinely linked to multiple poor health outcomes, and genuinely correctable with a cheap supplement. K2 is included for cardiovascular co-benefit when D is supplemented at higher doses. Test serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, dose accordingly, retest. Aim for 40-60 ng/mL.

Omega-3 (EPA + DHA) Strong

The other boring foundational entry. Adequate omega-3 intake from fatty fish or supplementation is linked to better cardiovascular outcomes, brain health, and reduced inflammation. Most western diets are deficient. A serum omega-3 index above 8% is the goal. Cheap, well-tolerated, foundational.

Creatine Monohydrate Strong

The most thoroughly studied supplement in sports nutrition history. Increasing evidence of cognitive benefits as well. 5g per day. Cheap. Supports the resistance-training pillar that genuinely moves longevity outcomes. Should be on most adults' shelves.

Magnesium (Glycinate or Threonate) Strong

Widespread dietary insufficiency. Supports sleep, muscle recovery, and glucose regulation. Magnesium glycinate for general use, magnesium L-threonate if cognitive support is the goal. 200-400 mg per day. Cheap, foundational.

III. The Honest Answer to "Best Longevity Supplements"

If a friend asked us tomorrow what to actually take, the answer for most adults would be: vitamin D + K2, omega-3, creatine, magnesium, and a quality multivitamin to cover gaps. Total cost roughly thirty dollars a month. The expected benefit, measured in healthspan years, exceeds anything the more exotic compounds can currently demonstrate.

The exotic compounds are not useless; they are just not yet earning a place in a routine. They earn that place by accumulating human evidence, by being shown to provide marginal benefit on top of the foundational practices, and by passing the opportunity-cost question for the specific reader. None of that is true at the moment for most of them.

IV. The Things That Beat Every Supplement

It is worth saying clearly. The interventions with the largest effect sizes on biological age and healthspan are not supplements. They are:

  1. Sleep — seven to nine hours, consistent, dark, cool, no alcohol within three hours of bed.
  2. Resistance training — two to four hours per week, progressive, heavy enough to challenge.
  3. Cardiovascular fitness — Zone 2 work plus weekly high-intensity intervals; the goal is VO2 max in the top quartile for age.
  4. Whole-food protein-adequate nutrition — 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight; high fiber; minimal ultra-processed food.
  5. Social connection and purpose — chronic loneliness carries mortality risk comparable to smoking.

These five outperform every supplement currently sold and most that are not yet sold. Any supplement decision should be made on top of these foundations, not instead of them. V. A Practical Decision Process

  1. Get a baseline. Comprehensive blood panel including fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid panel, hsCRP, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, omega-3 index, ferritin, B12. Add a biological age test if budget allows.
  2. Correct the obvious deficiencies first. Vitamin D, omega-3, B12, magnesium. These often produce visible improvements on retest.
  3. Optimize the five pillars for ninety days. Most "supplement-shaped" problems are sleep, training, or nutrition problems wearing supplement costumes.
  4. Retest. See what moved without exotic intervention. Most metrics improve substantially.
  5. Then, if you choose, add one supplement at a time, with a documented reason and a follow-up retest. Never add four at once; you will not know which one did what.

The longevity supplement question is best answered with discipline and patience. Both are also the deeper longevity intervention.
