What Is Contemplative Prayer: A Practical Guide

The oldest method of prayer, the great teachers, and how to begin tomorrow morning.

Contemplative prayer is the deepest and most undertaught form of Christian prayer. It is also one of the simplest. This guide explains what it actually is, distinguishes it from secular meditation, walks through the teachings of the great masters (John of the Cross, Teresa of Ávila, Thomas Merton, Thomas Keating), and gives you a practical method you can begin tomorrow.

I. What Contemplative Prayer Is

Contemplative prayer is the prayer of silent presence. It is the form of prayer in which you stop talking and listen — and in which, eventually, you stop even listening and simply rest in God's company. The Catechism gives a famous definition: contemplative prayer is "a gaze of faith fixed on Jesus, an attentiveness to the Word of God, a silent love." Three terms: gaze, attentiveness, silent love. None of them require words.

This is older than the modern interest in meditation by about two thousand years. The Desert Fathers and Mothers of the third and fourth centuries practiced it. Saint Benedict's monks were doing it in the sixth century. The medieval mystics — Bernard of Clairvaux, Hugh of Saint Victor, the anonymous author of The Cloud of Unknowing, John of the Cross, Teresa of Ávila — refined and described it. It went underground in the Catholic world for several centuries, was recovered by Thomas Merton and Thomas Keating in the twentieth century, and is now practiced by Catholics and other Christians around the world.

II. How It Differs from Meditation

The word meditation is used in three quite different senses today, and confusing them keeps a lot of seekers stuck.

  • Secular mindfulness meditation trains attention as a faculty. The goal is awareness itself, often for psychological or productivity benefits. There is no addressee.
  • Christian meditation in the traditional sense — sometimes called lectio or discursive meditation — uses the mind actively to ponder a passage of Scripture or a mystery of the faith, drawing the heart into prayer.
  • Contemplation — also called contemplative prayer, or the prayer of quiet — moves beyond active mental work into wordless presence. It is the natural progression of meditation when the mind quiets and the heart simply rests in God.

The simplest distinction: secular meditation is about you. Christian meditation is about thinking with God. Contemplative prayer is about being with God. The first is a practice. The second is a conversation. The third is a relationship.

III. The Great Teachers

John of the Cross (1542-1591)

The Spanish Carmelite friar who wrote the definitive map of the contemplative path, especially the dark night. His Ascent of Mount Carmel and Dark Night of the Soul are difficult but indispensable. If you read only one teacher, read him.

"In the evening of life, we will be judged on love alone."— Saint John of the Cross

Teresa of Ávila (1515-1582)

John's older contemporary and the reformer of the Carmelite order. Her Interior Castle describes the soul as a castle with seven mansions, each deeper than the last. Her writing is warmer, funnier, and more accessible than John's; her depth is equal.

"Let nothing disturb you. Let nothing frighten you. All things are passing away: God never changes."— Saint Teresa of Ávila

The Cloud of Unknowing (14th century, anonymous)

An English text by an anonymous monk that gave us the foundational image: between the soul and God there is a "cloud of unknowing" through which only love can pierce. The book is short, direct, and surprisingly modern in its psychological acuity.

Thomas Merton (1915-1968)

The American Trappist monk who reintroduced contemplative prayer to a wide twentieth-century audience. New Seeds of Contemplation is the best single entry to his work. His The Seven Storey Mountain remains one of the great conversion memoirs.

"Prayer is not asking. It is a longing of the soul."— Thomas Merton

Thomas Keating (1923-2018)

The Trappist abbot who developed the Centering Prayer method in the 1970s as a practical re-entry to contemplation for ordinary lay Christians. His method is simple enough to teach in five minutes and deep enough to last a lifetime. We will use it below.

IV. A Practical Method (Centering Prayer)

Centering Prayer, as Keating taught it, is built on four simple guidelines.

  1. Choose a sacred word as a symbol of your consent to God's presence and action within you. One or two syllables. Jesus, Abba, peace, love, silence. The word is not a mantra; it is a placeholder for your intention.
  2. Sit comfortably with eyes closed, settle briefly, and silently introduce the sacred word as the symbol of your consent.
  3. When you become aware of thoughts, return ever so gently to the sacred word. Thoughts are inevitable. They are not failure. The return is the prayer.
  4. At the end of the prayer period, remain in silence with eyes closed for a couple of minutes before resuming activity.

Twenty minutes is the standard length. Twice a day is the standard frequency (typically before breakfast and before dinner). If you can only do one twenty-minute session per day, do that. If you can only do ten minutes once, do that. Consistency beats duration.

V. What to Expect in the First Month

Almost everyone who begins contemplative prayer reports the same experience in the first weeks: distraction. The mind that has not been asked to be quiet in years does not become quiet on demand. Thoughts, lists, plans, anxieties, memories, replays of conversations — they all surface. This is normal and unavoidable.

The traditional teaching is clear: distraction is not failure, and trying to suppress thoughts only generates more thoughts. The practice is the gentle return. Each time you notice your attention has wandered, return to the sacred word. Twenty minutes of returning is twenty minutes of praying. The metric is not how quiet your mind became; it is whether you sat down at all.

After a few weeks, the texture shifts. Distractions still arise but lose their grip. Periods of quiet open up between them. Eventually — sometimes after months, sometimes after years — the prayer simplifies into a steady, undramatic presence. This is what the tradition calls the prayer of quiet.

VI. Where It Leads

Contemplative prayer is not a self-improvement program, and the people who try to make it one rarely stay. It is a relationship that deepens. Most people who pray contemplatively for a year or more report the same set of changes — not in the prayer itself, but in the rest of their lives. They are less reactive. They sleep better. They notice more. They listen differently. They become harder to bait into pointless conflict. They become, slowly, themselves.

The mystics promise much more than this, and they are not wrong. But the early returns are these quiet ones, and they are real, and they are what the tradition trusts most.
